# Chapter 5: CPA Day — What to Have Ready

> **What this chapter is:** a packing list for your first paid visit with a CPA or other licensed tax professional, plus plain-English notes about what to expect so the visit stays calm. **What it is not:** tax advice, or a promise about what your professional will do — your professional's scope, pricing, and process are theirs; this chapter just makes *arriving prepared* easy.

## Why "CPA Day" exists at all

Somewhere in your first year, one of these happens:

- You make the first money that the IRS needs to hear about, and the hows-and-whens deserve a professional's eyes.
- You want to know which flavor of "count it when…" applies to your specific situation.
- A big item (a real purchase, an odd refund, a business you ran in two states) makes you *not want to guess*.

All three have the same answer: **a licensed professional.** CPA Day is the prettiest name we could give "the hour where guessing ends."

The workbook's role is dull and heroic at once: **make the hour cheap.** Professionals charge for time and mind-reading; you can eliminate most of both by walking in with organized facts.

## The packing list

Bring these seven things. Most are one-page-or-less, thanks to Chapters 1–4.

| # | What to bring | Where it comes from | Ready when… |
|---|---------------|---------------------|-------------|
| 1 | **Your one-page P&L, all-year view** | Your Yearly Tracker / Chapter 6 | Every month has four numbers |
| 2 | **Bank statements, full year** | Your bank app, one download | You can see every month's statement without scrolling forever |
| 3 | **The year folder of receipts** | Chapter 4's system | Months have folders; most items have dots |
| 4 | **The ledger (or the workbook copy)** | `docs/08-ledger-worksheet.md` or your spreadsheet | Six accounts, one per column, closed monthly |
| 5 | **A short questions list** | Written by you, before the visit | You can read it aloud in under 2 minutes |
| 6 | **The basics sheet: business identity** | See "the basics sheet," below | Name, EIN (if you have one), state, start date, bank/account info |
| 7 | **Anything weird, flagged** | Your Detail column + "???" marks | You can point to the oddballs in one sentence each |

Item 7 deserves its own note: **the weirdos are the visit.** Organized normal stuff lets the professional go fast; the weird stuff is what they're *for*. Own your weirdos — bring them last, bring them proud.

## The basics sheet — a one-pager about your business

Professionals need the same handful of facts every time. Write them once, bring them always:

```
BASICS SHEET
Business name:           ____________________
Owner name:              ____________________
Business structure:      _____ (e.g., LLC — formed on __ /__ /____)
State of formation:      _____
First money business made:  __ /__ /____  (approximate is fine; note it)
Business bank account:   bank name, last-4 of account
Who I expect to pay/what I worry about: one honest sentence
```

**Never build this from memory at the office.** Build it at your kitchen table the week before, write it down, and *keep it forever* — future CPA Days just add a new column.

## The questions list — the highest-leverage page you bring

Before the visit, write 3–6 actual questions. New solopreneurs often freeze at exactly this step, so here are common first-year starters to spark your own (these workbook examples are education prompts, not advice in either direction):

- "Cash basis is what my books use — is that workable for me?" *(Chapter 2 taught the words; they decide fit.)*
- "For my Set-Aside jar, is there a number for my situation?" *(The workbook never tells you YOUR %; they can.)*
- "Should the LLC's default treatment be my treatment? Tell me the options." *(That's a licensed-professional question by definition.)*
- "Month X had an odd refund — here's my note. Count it now or later?"
- "My structure: what do I owe this year, and to whom, roughly when?"
- "Anything in my ledger style that creates extra work for you? I'd rather fix my habit than your bill."

Sizes vary, but visits reliably cost *more* when a professional must reverse-engineer your year, and *less* when you arrive with this packing list.

## What a CPA Day actually feels like (so it stops being scary)

A first visit, honestly described:

1. **Handover (first 10 min)** — you give them the packing list; they read. Silence here is normal and *good*: it means they're working, not interrogating you.
2. **Questions back (10–30 min)** — they'll ask things like "was this $750 laptop new?" That's not a trap; it's the difference between the two accounts you learned first (Operating Expenses vs Big Purchases — see, the six accounts were CPA-Lingual practice all along).
3. **Plain-English verdicts (10–30 min)** — what your treatment is, which dates matter, roughly how much and when. **Write these down like your books depend on them, because they do.**
4. **The next steps (5 min)** — what they need from you, what happens month-by-month from here, when you reconnect.

Walking out with: verdicts for your ledger, dates for your calendar, and answers to your questions list — most of which the prep above shortened payably.

## After the CPAs go home: the update ritual

CPA Day ends with homework, and this workbook's turn is to fold the verdicts into your system in one sitting:

1. **Fix your ledger notes** — if they said a specific buy counts differently, write their reasoning on the ledger page, next to the line. (Your books stay educational-honest; their verdict stays visible.)
2. **Update the jar** — if they gave you a Set-Aside number, that replaces your starter habit starting now.
3. **Calendar the dates** — actual due dates get into your actual calendar, with a two-week early warning.
4. **Register the styling** — if they want a different account arrangement (a split, a rename, a fifth column on the worksheet), change the template once, not twelve monthly pages.

After that, year two's CPA Day tends to be a 20-minute check-in — which is the entire arc this workbook promised: a first year of books that starts in a shoebox and ends with a professional saying "this is clean, quick, done."

> **The closing bound (and we mean it always):** this workbook is education. Nothing in it — including this chapter, which mostly describes *how to behave at a professional's office* — is legal, accounting, or tax advice about your situation. The professional you visit gets paid to be the advice.
