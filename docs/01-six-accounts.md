# Chapter 1: The Six Accounts You Actually Need

> **What this chapter is:** a friendly tour of the six bookkeeping accounts that carry a first-year solopreneur almost all the way. **What it is not:** tax advice, or a lecture from someone who has never been nervous about money. Education only — talk to a licensed professional before making financial or tax decisions.

## Why six, and not fifty?

If you open a big company's bookkeeping software, you might see forty account names and want to close the laptop. You don't need forty. A first-year solo business needs a small set of *buckets* that answer the only four questions that matter:

1. Where is the money?
2. What came in?
3. What went out?
4. What's still owed or set aside?

Six accounts answer all four. You can always add a seventh later, once the books ask for it — but six is where you start, because six is a number you can hold in your head.

## The six accounts

| # | Account | Answers the question… | Think of it as… |
|---|---------|----------------------|------------------|
| 1 | **Business Checking** | "Where is the money?" | The business's wallet |
| 2 | **Income** | "What came in?" | The front door |
| 3 | **Operating Expenses** | "What went out for daily work?" | The regular grocery bill |
| 4 | **Big Purchases** | "What went out once, but lasts years?" | The occasional furniture buy |
| 5 | **Pay Me (Owner Draws)** | "How much did I take for myself?" | The trip home after work |
| 6 | **Set-Aside** | "What am I holding for taxman/surprises?" | A labeled jar on the shelf |

Let me walk through each one the way a friend would.

### 1. Business Checking

Not really a "bookkeeping account" in the ledger sense — it's a real bank account — but it's the spine of all six. If your personal and business money share one checking account, you will spend **hours** untangling them later, and every hour spent untangling is an hour not spent on the actual work.

Plain-English rule: **the business gets its own checking account, even if the business is just you and a laptop.** Everything in this workbook assumes it exists.

### 2. Income

One bucket for every dollar the business earns. Royalties, client invoices, eBay sales, ad share — all of it lands here first, then (on cash basis, see Chapter 2) it counts the day it arrives.

Why not split income into "KDP income," "consulting income," "marketplace sales" right away? Because in year one, a single Income account tells you honest monthly totals, and *that's what the P&L needs.* If you later want to know *where* income came from, you add a note in the ledger's Detail column — no new accounts required.

### 3. Operating Expenses

The everyday cost of running the show: software subscriptions, shipping supplies, the domain name, the AI-tool subscription you actually use for client work. Expenses you use up within the year belong here.

Kitchen-table test: *Would a sensible person say "that's just running the business"?* If yes, it goes here.

### 4. Big Purchases

Some buys don't get "used up" in the year — a $900 laptop, a standing desk, a printer that outlives three laptops. Traditionally these are called **assets** and they get "depreciated" (spread out over years by specific tax rules). You do **not** need to master depreciation in year one.

What you need in year one is honest separation: big long-lived things land in **Big Purchases** so they don't inflate your month-to-month Operating Expenses and scare you into thinking a quiet sales month was a disaster. When tax time comes, your CPA will tell you how those get treated (some big purchases can often be treated differently in the year bought — that's CPA territory, not workbook territory).

Kitchen-table test: *Will I still be using this a year from now, and did it cost real money?* If both, it's a Big Purchase.

> There's no magic dollar threshold in this workbook. Use your gut — the purpose is clarity, not perfection. When in doubt, ask your CPA; that's exactly why Chapter 5 has you keep the receipts.

### 5. Pay Me (Owner Draws)

When you take money out of the business for groceries and rent, that money is not an "expense" of the business — it's you getting paid. This account is the receipt for that. **Money moving from Business Checking to your personal life is a Draw** (a "distribution," in grown-up language).

Why this matters more than beginners expect: if you don't track Draws, two bad things happen. First, your expenses look mysteriously larger than they are. Second, you can lose track of what the business *actually cost* versus *how much you personally consumed*. The P&L (Chapter 6) only stays honest if Draws stay out of it.

### 6. Set-Aside

The "labeled jar" account: money you're deliberately holding so tax season and slow months don't ambush you. This isn't technically an *account category* in formal accounting — it's really just a slice of checking you've mentally earmarked — and that's fine. The psychological power of a labeled jar is enormous, and it costs nothing.

A friendly starting habit (educational example, not a rule or tax advice): when a client payment lands, move a slice — some solopreneurs use 25–30%, many simply pick a number they can live with — into the jar. The point is that *something predictable happens*, and your future self stops dreading April.

## What the six accounts look like on paper

Here's a full month in miniature, walked through account by account. Read it slowly — this table *is* the whole system:

| Day | What happened | Account touched | Withdrawn? | Added? |
|-----|---------------|-----------------|------------|--------|
| 3rd | Royalty payment $180 arrives in checking | Income + Checking | — | $180 |
| 5th | $14.99 software subscription auto-charged | Operating Expenses | $14.99 | — |
| 9th | Moved $50 to the tax jar | Set-Aside | $50* | $50 |
| 12th | Bought a $750 laptop | Big Purchases | $750 | — |
| 18th | Client paid $400 (invoiced last month) | Income + Checking | — | $400 |
| 21st | Transferred $300 to personal for rent | Pay Me | $300 | — |
| 26th | $32 shipping supplies | Operating Expenses | $32 | — |

\* The $50 "moved" to Set-Aside isn't spent — it's still the business's money, just labeled. That's the jar working.

Notice what did *not* happen: no new spreadsheets, no categories-within-categories, no app subscriptions. Six buckets, seven lines, one honest month on one honest page.

## The one-week setup, step by step

1. **Open (or designate) a business checking account.** Everything else depends on this one.
2. **Create the ledger with the six accounts as column headers** — or use the fill-in worksheet in `docs/08-ledger-worksheet.md` if you like paper.
3. **Pick a jar amount.** Any predictable slice. Write it on the front of the ledger.
4. **Do your first monthly close** (Chapter 3) even if the numbers are tiny. The ritual is the skill; the numbers are just the ingredients.

## Common first-year worries, answered plainly

**"What if I use a wrong account?"** Move it, note it in the Detail column, move on. A self-correcting system beats a perfect one you're scared to touch.

**"Do the six accounts work with bookkeeping software?"** Beautifully. Software categories map onto these six; the ledger just translates.

**"What if I have savings interest, or refunds?"** Small items land in the closest fitting account with a Detail note. The system bends. It does not shatter.

**"Is this real accounting?"** It's simplified, honest, and consistent — which is exactly what a first-year small business needs. Formal double-entry bookkeeping exists if you ever grow into it; your six accounts translate directly.

> **Remember:** this workbook is education. Your situation — including state rules, your industry, and anything tax-related — deserves a licensed professional's eyes. The workbook's job is to make that visit affordable, quick, and calm, not to replace it.
