# Chapter 6: Reading a One-Page P&L Without Fear

> **What this chapter is:** the skill of reading a single page of numbers and knowing, honestly, whether the month was OK. **What it is not:** finance advice, forecasting training, or an invitation to optimize your life into a dashboard.

## What a P&L actually is (and why solopreneurs get scarier versions)

**P&L** means **Profit and Loss** — a page that says: *money came in, money went out, did we end up ahead?* Big companies call longer versions "statements of operations" and hire people to read them. Yours fits on one page, and reading it takes less effort than making breakfast:

- **Income** — what landed
- **Expenses** — what left (Operating Expenses, plus Big Purchases shown separately)
- **Net profit** — the difference

That's the page. Everything else — accounting-speak, KPI's, dashboard widgets — is decoration your first-year business doesn't need, and Chapter 3 already taught you where the numbers come from (Phase 3 of the monthly close: foot the columns).

## Your one-page P&L, on the page

Here's an example-month format, which is also on the fill-in worksheet so you can copy it:

```
            P&L — MARCH  — Solopreneur, cash basis (education example)
  +-------------------------------+---------+
  | INCOME                        |         |
  |   Money that landed           |  $580   |
  +-------------------------------+---------+
  | OPERATING EXPENSES            |         |
  |   Software & subscriptions    |  $ 45   |
  |   Supplies & shipping         |  $ 38   |
  |   Small misc (2 items)        |  $ 17   |
  |   Subtotal                    |  $100   |
  +-------------------------------+---------+
  | BIG PURCHASES                 |  $750   |   <- laptop this month
  +-------------------------------+---------+
  | NET PROFIT (cash)             | -$270   |   (!)
  +-------------------------------+---------+
  | (not P&L lines, for your eye) |         |
  |   Pay Me (owner draws)        |  $300   |   (not an expense)
  |   Set-Aside jar moved         |  $120   |   (not an expense)
  +-------------------------------+---------+
```

## Reading it in three moves, not thirty

**Move 1: Is Net Profit positive?**
Start at the bottom. Positive number → month paid for itself. Negative → month drew down savings/float. Either way, that's the month's one-paragraph story; the rest is detail.

**Move 2: Are the ingredients sane?**
Skim top to bottom asking a friendlier version of "does this look right?":
- If Operating Expenses doubled out of nowhere, which line did it: new subscription? Two months of a bill cleared together? Your eye finds it in seconds; the numbers only need to be *roughly* familiar, not perfect.
- Big Purchases spiking one month is *supposed* to spike that month's profit down. (See "the laptop trap," below, before you panic.)

**Move 3: Compare to *last month*, not to an imaginary business.**
The P&L's secret skill is **second-order**: not "was this a good number?" but "differently how?" A stay-flat month in a stable business is a win. The comparison lives in your Yearly Tracker, which is why Chapter 3 has you write all four numbers there every month.

## The two rookie reading mistakes — and their antidotes

### The laptop trap (Big Purchases vs Operating Expenses)

March's P&L above shows **-$270**. Terror? Look again: the $750 laptop is a slow-lived purchase that most likely won't repeat this year, while $100 of Operating Expenses is the real monthly cost of running the thing. Here's the same month's P&L with the laptop separately visible — this is exactly why Chapter 1 made Big Purchases its own bucket:

```
  NET, ignoring Big Purchases:    $580 - $100 = +$480  <- the running month
  IN ADDITION, one-time:          bought a laptop for 750
  NET with laptop:                -$270  <- the month in the bank
```

Amateurs read one number. You now read **two**: the *running* month (Operating-based) and the *bank* month (with Big Purchases included). Both are honest; they answer different questions — "could I sustain this month forever?" (running) versus "what happened to checking?" (bank). Beginners compare to 0; owners read the pair.

### The pay-yourself confusion (Draws aren't expenses)

The bottom two lines — Pay Me $300, jar $120 — never affect profit. Say it until it sticks, because the reverse myth is the most persistent novice myth in solopreneurship: **drawing a salary is not an expense.** If March's profit were $480 while *also* Pay Me'ing $300, the business covered work costs of $100 *plus* bankrolled YOU $300 *plus* parked $120. That's a strong month wearing a weak costume — the pair-of-readings habit (Move 1, plus your eye for the bottom block) is exactly how you spot one.

## Reading around the timing wobble (cash-basis P&Ls only)

Since Chapter 2: cash basis books say income on the day it *lands*, so a bad-luck month (big project invoiced late-October, paid mid-November…) shows an October that looks like a miss and a November that looks like a spike. Reading advice, friend-variety:

- **Don't fix this in the ledgers.** The ledger's honesty is its spoke; no "accrual adjuster" improvisations in a first-year workbook.
- **Read with month-pairs.** Tracker columns let you see, for example, "moved a project's payday from Oct to Nov" at a glance.
- **Read quarter-level for drama-free conclusions.** Few people sign big checks fast; almost nobody does it thrice wrong in a row. 3 months together is much closer to a real business's story than any one month.

If timing wobble makes a *single* month scary, the rule of thumb you're allowed to use: **read two more months before deciding anything** (a phrase borrowed from the whole of finance, friend-version).

## The P&L's bottom line vs "did the business get healthier?"

A genuinely useful last question. Cash profit is a poor proxy for health when it's your first year (timing wobble, big buys, startup spending). Owners tend to look at three cheap health-checks instead:

1. **Are the months' running-profits (as above) at least near zero at the median?** No Narration drama: direction beats magnitude in year one.
2. **Is the jar's slice actually happening?** — meaning you are *buffered*, which is the real health test for a solo business.
3. **Did receipts/ledger feel boring-to-maintain this month?** — calm books predict a calm CPA Day (Chapter 5); chaotic books predict a chaotic one.

That's 3 yes/no questions, no dashboards, and honestly more informative than most charts.

## Your monthly two-minute read

After Phase 3 of your close, spend two minutes talking to yourself on paper:

1. Net profit number, and running-month number.
2. Anything on the page that surprised me? (Which line?)
3. Jar got its slice, yes/no.
4. One sentence about how this month *felt* money-wise. (Same line Chapter 3 asks for — one number, one feeling, every month. That's a P&L habit.)

An excerpt of March in plain-English, if it helps to see one:

> "March ended -$270 in the bank because of the $750 laptop, but the running month was +$480, jar got $120, nothing surprised me. Verdict: normal plus."
