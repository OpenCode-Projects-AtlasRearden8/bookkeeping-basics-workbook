# Chapter 2: Cash Basis, in Plain English

> **What this chapter is:** one idea, explained like a friend would, with kitchen-table examples. **What it is not:** tax advice. Whether cash-basis accounting is *allowed* for your business depends on IRS rules for your situation — ask a licensed professional. This workbook teaches the idea so you can follow along when they advise you.

## The one-sentence version

**Cash basis means money counts when it actually moves, not when it's promised.**

That's the whole concept. Everything else in this chapter is just that sentence, stretched out patiently.

## Two dates in every story

Say you do consulting work for a client in **October**. You invoice them October 30th. They pay you **November 12th**. Your books have to pick a date to say "this income happened." There are only two choices:

| Method | Income counts on… | In our story… |
|--------|-------------------|---------------|
| **Accrual** (what many bigger companies use) | When the work happened — the invoice goes out | October 30th |
| **Cash basis** (the solopreneur-friendly one) | When the money lands | November 12th |

Cash basis says: **the money's arrival date is the event.** You didn't *have* the money in October. In November, you did. Done.

The same logic runs backwards for expenses: on cash basis, an **expense counts when you paid it**, not when you got the bill or when the service was technically rendered.

## Why first-year solopreneurs almost always prefer it

1. **It matches your bank statement.** On cash basis, your one-page P&L essentially narrates your checking account. When the bank says $180 arrived on the 3rd, your books say $180 arrived on the 3rd. Reconciling is nearly automatic.

2. **No accounting software required.** Accrual accounting needs you to track "accounts receivable" (people who owe you) and "accounts payable" (people you owe) as formal ledger accounts. Cash basis just needs neurons and a notebook.

3. **The dates you know perfectly are the real dates.** You know exactly when money cleared — the bank tells you, timestamped. Do you know the *work-date* of every invoice? Most people don't, at the end of a long week.

The one real cost of cash basis: on paper, a month where you did lots of work but got paid late can look like a famine, and the following month looks like a feast. Section "Reading around the timing wobble" in Chapter 6 shows how to handle that without panic.

## Money in: the four ways dollars arrive

| Way it arrives | Cash-basis date | Which account |
|----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Client or platform payment lands in checking | The day it lands | **Income** |
| A customer refund you receive (you overpaid a vendor) | The day it lands | Income, with a Detail note |
| Money you lend *to* the business and it comes back | The day it lands back | Detail note — not Income |
| You move money TO the business from personal | Never Income! | Detail note — not Income! |

That last row is the most common first-year mistake on earth: transferring $500 of your personal savings into the business and accidentally counting it as Income. It isn't income. It's a **loan to yourself**, and it comes out of *Owner Draws*' mirror side (an owner contribution), not Income. Your Set-Aside jar's honesty depends on not making this mistake.

## Money out: the three flavors of spending

| Flavor | Cash-basis date | Which account |
|--------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Operating expenses** (software, supplies, postage) | Day paid | Operating Expenses |
| **Big purchases** (laptop, desk — long-lived things) | Day paid | Big Purchases |
| **Paying yourself** | Day of transfer to personal | Pay Me (Owner Draws) |

One more flavor deserves its own note: **paying for something in advance** — like paying for next year's software subscription in December. Cash basis says: day paid. Deeper questions about "when does this really count" are exactly CPA territory; you just need the day-paid habit for your own calm books.

## What "paid" means, when the bank is slow

Small honest wrinkle: a payment you *initiated* on the 29th might *clear* on the 31st or the 1st. On cash basis, use the clear date — the date the money actually left. Public practice: most first-year solopreneurs write down the date they see on the bank statement. That's the same muscle, and it keeps books and bank statements in agreement, which is worth more than theoretical precision.

## A worked example, one week live

Here's a week, narrated, then bottled in the ledger format from Chapter 1:

- **Monday.** Client work. Nothing arrives. Nothing recorded. (While cash basis feels "slow" on days like this, remember — it isn't recording *work*, it's recording *cash*. Work has other trackers.)
- **Tuesday.** Your $14.99 software subscription renews. Checking drops. -> Operating Expenses, $14.99, Tuesday.
- **Wednesday.** Old invoice from last month pays out — $400 in checking. -> Income, $400, Wednesday. (Not last month. Wednesday.)
- **Thursday.** You transfer $150 to personal account for groceries. -> Pay Me, $150, Thursday.
- **Friday.** You buy $28 in shipping supplies. -> Operating Expenses, $28, Friday.

Week's total profit, cash basis: +$400 - $14.99 - $28 = **+$357.01**. One sentence summary, no spreadsheets harmed.

## Cash basis in six sentences

If you remember nothing else from this workbook:

1. Money counts when it moves.
2. Income = the day money lands in business checking.
3. Expenses = the day money leaves business checking.
4. Paying yourself is never an expense — always a Draw.
5. Contributions *you make to* the business are never income.
6. If you're ever unsure which flavor of "when," write the bank date and move on.

> **The disclaimer corner:** This chapter teaches what cash-basis accounting *means*. It does not tell you your business is entitled to use it, or that any year is handled the way you might wish. That's a conversation with a licensed professional. Bring your ledger from this workbook — it'll make their answer fast and cheap.
